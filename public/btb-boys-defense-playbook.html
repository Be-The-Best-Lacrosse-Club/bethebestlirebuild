Defense Is Communication

Talent wins matchups. Communication wins games. A defense that talks knows where help is coming from before the dodge starts. The calls below are the BTB defensive language. Every player uses them, every possession, at every age level. Every tab in this playbook uses this same vocabulary, and every clip is NCAA Division I men's film.

BTB Defensive Communication System

"BALL! BALL! BALL!"
I have the ball carrier. Say it three times so the whole field knows who is on-ball. If nobody calls ball, the defense has already broken down.
"HOT!"
I am the first slide. If the on-ball defender gets beat, I leave my man to stop the ball. Everyone else rotates behind me.
"TWO!"
I am the second slide. When Hot leaves, I cover the man he left. This chain reaction keeps the defense connected.
"HELP LEFT!" / "HELP RIGHT!"
Told to the on-ball defender by the slider. It says which side the help is on so he can force the dodger into it.
"PICK LEFT!" / "PICK RIGHT!"
A screen is coming. Called by the screener's defender the moment the picker starts moving, not after contact. Give your teammate two to three seconds of warning.
"THROUGH!" / "SWITCH!"
Our answer to the pick. Through means fight over the top and keep your man. Switch means swap. Both defenders must say it back. A one-sided switch is an open shot.
"CUTTER!"
An off-ball attacker is moving through the lane. Whoever sees it calls it, bumps him, and passes him to the next defender.
"DEAD SIDE!"
The ball is on the far side of the field from my man. I am sagged into the hole, eyes on ball and man, ready to bump cutters or be the second slide.
"CHECK!"
Everyone find your man. Used after a fast break, dead ball, or scramble. Before the next whistle every defender has eyes on his assignment.

Duke Staff on Defensive Communication

Coach's Playbook: The Duke staff (John Danowski, Ron Caputo, Matt Danowski, Ned Crotty) answer a question on defensive talk. Their standard is talking and listening: see the man and hear the man, head on a swivel, and an off-ball stance that lets you act on the call the instant you hear it.
Key Teaching Point: Communication is a two-way skill. We grade our defenders on whether they hear and respond to calls, not only on whether they make them.

Key Defensive Terms

Adjacent Slide: The defender next to the on-ball defender slides first. Shortest distance, fastest help, but it opens the adjacent attacker for a quick pass.
Crease Slide: The defender on the crease attacker slides first. Longer distance but it protects the most dangerous spot on the field. Used against a dominant crease attackman.
Near Man: The closest defender slides regardless of assignment. Harder to scout, but every player must read and call it in real time.
Hedge: A short jump-out by the slider to slow the ball carrier while the on-ball defender recovers. The hedger shows and retreats. He does not commit.
Recovery: Getting back to a man after a slide or rotation. Recovery speed decides whether the defense gives up a shot. Sprint, never jog.
Bump: Legal body contact that redirects a cutter moving through the lane.
Split: One defender guarding two attackers who are both one pass from the ball. He sits between them and takes whichever one receives it.

Slides & Recovery

No defender stops every dodge alone. Help must come. The questions are where it comes from, how fast it arrives, and what happens after. Every slide creates an open man. The defense's job is to recover before the offense finds him. Watch how Notre Dame, Maryland, Georgetown, and Yale answer those questions in real games.

Defending Picks

The two-man game is the most common action in organized D1 offense. Every defense needs a plan for it. Our three answers are Through (fight over the top), Switch (swap men), and Hedge (the screener's defender shows and recovers). Which one we use depends on where the pick is set and who is setting it.

Key Teaching Point: The screener's defender is the communicator. He sees the pick first because his man is setting it. Late call, no call, and open shot are the same thing.

Off-Ball Defense & Help

Off-ball cutters are the silent killers of team defense. While everyone watches the ball, a good attackman cuts through the lane, gets behind his man, and finishes a feed. Defending it takes three things: the ball-you-man triangle, bump responsibility, and weak-side vision.

The Ball-You-Man Triangle

The Rule: Position yourself so you can see the ball and your man without turning your head more than 90 degrees. If you have to spin to find one of them, you are out of position.
One Pass Away: Deny position. Half a body between your man and the ball, stick in the passing lane, ready to contest the pass or be Hot.
Two Passes Away ("Dead Side"): Sag toward the crease and the ball. You protect the middle, bump cutters, and are the second slide.
A Cutter Enters Your Zone: Call "Cutter," bump him, and pass him to the next defender. It is a relay. You own your zone, not the cutter.

Man-Down Defense

Five against six means somebody is always open. The question is how long it takes the offense to find him. Good man-down defense makes them throw extra passes, contests every shot, and steals possessions. Our base is the Box-and-1. Our change-up is pressure.

BTB Man-Down Packages

Box-and-1 (base): Four defenders in a box that rotates with the ball, one locked on the crease. Simplest to teach and hardest for young EMO units to break. Install this first.
Pressure: Get out to the ball carrier's hands and speed him up while the backside collapses. Use it once or twice a game when the opponent's EMO has found a rhythm, not as your base.
Surprise Lock: Show the box for ten to fifteen seconds, then on a sideline call lock man-to-man. Pre-assign the matchups before the penalty. Film for this look is still being sourced.

3-3 Zone Defense

Zone is a weapon, not a fallback. Three defenders across the top and three on the crease build a wall that takes away inside shots and forces the offense to beat you from the perimeter. It is especially effective against dodging teams because there is nobody to dodge. The zone shifts to the ball, not to a man. At BTB it is a change of pace we call from the sideline when the opponent is in rhythm against our man-to-man.

Zone Terminology

Shift: All six defenders move the same direction on every pass. This is what keeps the zone intact.
Seam: The gap between two zones. The offense's whole plan is to find and attack it.
Pinch: Ball behind the cage. Crease defenders squeeze the goal, top defenders sag into the middle, force a perimeter shot.
Match-Up Zone: Zone principles, but defenders lock the nearest attacker when the ball enters their area.
Trap Zone: Two defenders double the ball in a designated area (usually the wing) while the other four collapse inside. Only works if all four collapse together.
Zone Buster: The attacker who sits in a seam and forces two defenders to decide who has him. Communicate to deny him the ball.
